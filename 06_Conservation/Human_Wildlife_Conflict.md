# Human–Wildlife Conflict

As human populations expand into wild landscapes, interactions between people and large carnivores become more frequent.  These interactions often result in conflict, particularly when cats prey on livestock or are perceived as threats to safety.  Conflict can lead to retaliatory killings and undermine conservation efforts.

## Causes of conflict

1. **Livestock predation:** Tigers, pumas, lions and snow leopards sometimes kill cattle, goats or horses【671062261146697†L245-L265】【596547831032212†L248-L286】.  When prey is scarce, even caracals and servals may raid poultry or small stock【514282553040283†L176-L183】.
2. **Habitat encroachment:** Human settlements and farms fragment habitats, bringing people and predators into closer contact【960819529586131†L75-L104】.
3. **Perceived threat:** Fear of attacks can lead to pre‑emptive killing of predators, even if actual risk to people is low.

## Mitigation strategies

- **Livestock management:** Using predator‑proof enclosures, guard animals and herding practices can reduce losses.  Timely carcass removal discourages scavenging.
- **Compensation schemes:** Governments and NGOs provide financial compensation for livestock lost to predators, reducing the need for revenge killing.
- **Community engagement:** Education programmes and community‑based conservation (e.g. snow leopard enterprises) empower local people to protect wildlife while benefiting economically.
- **Translocation and deterrents:** Relocating problem animals or using non‑lethal deterrents (lights, noise) can mitigate conflicts.

Successful programmes demonstrate that coexistence is possible when both ecological and socio‑economic factors are addressed.
