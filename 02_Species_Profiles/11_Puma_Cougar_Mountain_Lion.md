# Puma / Cougar / Mountain Lion

> **The ghost cat of the mountains, traversing the absolute length of the Americas with Olympic athletic power**

## 1. Core Identity

- **Common name:** Puma, cougar, mountain lion
- **Alternative names:** Panther, catamount, painter; Guinness World Record holder for the animal with the highest number of names.
- **Scientific name:** _Puma concolor_
- **Taxonomic group:** Subfamily _Felinae_; genus _Puma_
- **Lineage:** Puma lineage (shared with the cheetah)
- **Exhibit role:** American cat exhibit
- **Main biome:** Wide range including forests, mountains, deserts and grasslands
- **Main hunting style:** Ambush predator with powerful leaps

## 2. Museum Summary

Padding silently along a red rock canyon ledge in the American Southwest, or slipping through the mist-shrouded peaks of the Andes, a master of adaptability surveys its domain. This is the **puma**—also famously known as the cougar, mountain lion, or panther. Holding the Guinness World Record for the animal with the most names, this magnificent feline possesses the widest latitudinal range (the geographic distance north and south of the equator) of any wild land mammal in the Western Hemisphere, thriving in almost every habitat from the sub-zero forests of the Canadian Yukon down to the windswept grasslands of Patagonia.

Despite its impressive size, which matches or exceeds that of some big cats, the puma is anatomically a member of the "small cat" subfamily, meaning it purrs like a housecat instead of roaring. Its defining physical superpower lies in its extraordinary, elongated hind legs. These powerful limbs enable the puma to leap an astonishing **6 metres** vertically into the air or launch itself **12 metres** horizontally to surprise prey. Operating as a silent, solitary ambush specialist, the puma stalks deer and guanacos before delivering a crushing strike, serving as a vital keystone species that maintains the ecological health of the entire American continent.

## 3. Range

- **Continents:** North America and South America.
- **Countries/regions:** Extends from western Canada and the western United States through Mexico and Central America, all the way to the southern tip of Chile and Argentina.
- **Range type:** Expansive but highly fragmented in densely populated areas; widely extirpated (locally wiped out) from the eastern half of North America, with the exception of a critically endangered relict population in Florida (the Florida panther).
- **Range notes:** Its extreme geographical distribution means the puma lives in more distinct biomes than almost any other land predator.

## 4. Habitat

- **Primary habitat:** Rugged, mountainous regions, coniferous alpine forests, and dense tropical rainforests.
- **Secondary habitat:** Arid deserts, coastal scrublands, flooded wetlands, and open grasslands near shelter.
- **Climate adaptation:** Adaptable coat thickness; northern populations grow a thick, silver-gray coat in winter, while tropical populations maintain a sleek, reddish-brown summer coat.
- **Terrain preference:** Heavily broken, rocky terrain containing cliffs, canyons, and dense brush that provides stalking cover.

## 5. Diet

- **Main prey:** Large wild ungulates (hoofed mammals) such as mule deer, white-tailed deer, elk, moose, and guanacos.
- **Secondary prey:** Smaller mammals (including jackrabbits, beavers, and porcupines), coyotes, birds, and occasionally domestic livestock.
- **Hunting method:** Close-range ambush. The puma stalks silently through rocks or trees, utilizing its tawny coat as perfect camouflage, then leaps from behind cover to land directly on the prey's back, breaking the neck or delivering a suffocating throat bite.
- **Feeding ecology:** Master of food storage; they will drag large kills into secluded ravines, cover them with dry leaves, twigs, or snow (caching), and return to feed over several days.

## 6. Behaviour / Ecology

- **Social structure:** Strictly solitary, avoiding other adults except during brief mating windows. Females raise their litters of 1–4 spotted cubs in safe caves or rocky clefts for up to two years.
- **Activity rhythm:** Primarily nocturnal (night-active) and crepuscular (active at dawn and dusk) to avoid daytime human activity.
- **Territory:** Establish vast home ranges; a single male's territory can span over 300 square kilometres and typically overlaps the ranges of several breeding females.
- **Ecological role:** Primary apex predator regulating deer and herbivore populations, which helps protect local riverbeds and vegetation from overgrazing.

## 7. Build & Scale

- **Body size:** Males **1.8–2.4 m** (6–8 ft) in total length including the tail; tail adds **66–96 cm** (2.2–3.1 ft).
- **Weight range:** Males **50–80 kg** (110–180 lb); females **35–60 kg** (77–132 lb).
- **Key proportions:** Extremely long hind limbs that are noticeably longer than the forelimbs; a small head with a black-and-white muzzle; a long, heavy tail tipped with a dark brush.
- **Movement style:** Athletic and silent; capable of climbing steep cliffs, leaping vast chasms, and sprinting in short, explosive bursts.

## 8. Signature Traits

1. **The ultimate spring:** Elongated hind legs that power vertical leaps of up to 6 metres, allowing the cat to jump onto high rocky ledges.
2. **Guinness name champion:** Known by over 40 distinct names in English alone, reflecting its enormous cultural impact across indigenous and modern societies.
3. **The quiet throat:** Possesses a rigid hyoid bone that prevents roaring but allows it to produce high-pitched screams, growls, and loud purrs.
4. **Tawny camouflage:** A solid, unpatterned coat ranging from warm gold to cool gray that acts like a blank canvas, perfectly blending into rocks and forests.

## 9. Conservation

- **IUCN status:** Near Threatened.
- **Population trend:** Decreasing overall, though rebounding in several protected western states of the US.
- **Main threats:** Habitat fragmentation by highways, retaliatory killings by livestock owners, vehicle collisions, and sport hunting.
- **Conservation notes:** Crucial efforts focus on building wildlife crossings (vegetated highway bridges) and educating ranchers about non-lethal livestock guard options.
