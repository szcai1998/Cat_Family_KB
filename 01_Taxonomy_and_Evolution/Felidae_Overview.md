# Felidae Overview

The **Felidae** are a family of carnivorous mammals commonly known as cats.  Felids are slender, flexible and muscular hypercarnivores adapted for stealthy hunting【943641874497049†L60-L76】.  They range in size from the tiny African black‑footed cat to the enormous tiger, yet share a recognisable body plan: large eyes for night vision, round and flat faces, sensitive whiskers, powerful jaws, retractable claws and a flexible spine【943641874497049†L60-L76】【943641874497049†L79-L97】.  Felids walk on their toes (digitigrade stance) and their claws retract into protective sheaths when not in use【943641874497049†L123-L139】.  The exception is the cheetah, whose claws are only partially retractable to provide extra grip at high speed【943641874497049†L136-L140】.

## Classification

Living felids are grouped into two primary **subfamilies**: **_Pantherinae_** (the big cats) and **_Felinae_** (small and medium cats).  According to a modern taxonomy compiled from sources such as the Cat Classification Task Force, the _Pantherinae_ include the genera _Panthera_ (tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard and snow leopard) and _Neofelis_ (clouded leopards).  All other extant species belong to the subfamily _Felinae_【943641874497049†L60-L67】.  In total there are roughly 41 wild felid species across eleven genera【943641874497049†L60-L67】.  Domestic cats (_Felis catus_) descend from wildcats and are part of _Felinae_.

### Big cats and small cats

Historically the term **“big cat”** referred to the four species that could roar (lion, tiger, jaguar and leopard).  Modern usage often includes the snow leopard and clouded leopards as well【943641874497049†L69-L73】.  “Small cats” encompasses the remaining species in _Felinae_ such as the cheetah, puma, lynx, serval, caracal, ocelot and many others【943641874497049†L60-L67】.  Big cats tend to be larger and occupy the top of food webs, but the division is rooted in anatomy: big cats have a flexible ligament in the hyoid apparatus that allows most of them to roar, whereas small cats have a fully ossified hyoid and can purr continuously.

### Distribution

Felids naturally occur on every continent except Antarctica and Australia【943641874497049†L74-L78】.  Their highest diversity is found in Asia and Africa, but species such as the jaguar and puma range across the Americas.  Many felids are habitat specialists: snow leopards patrol high mountains, servals stalk wet savannas, and pallas’s cats hide among Central Asian rocks.  Others, like leopards and pumas, are highly adaptable generalists.

## Adaptations for predation

All felids are ambush hunters.  Their musculoskeletal system emphasises short bursts of speed and agility rather than endurance running【943641874497049†L86-L101】.  Powerful hind limbs provide explosive acceleration and allow cats to pounce or leap great distances.  A floating clavicle (collar bone) enables cats to squeeze through tight spaces by allowing the shoulders to collapse inward【943641874497049†L98-L103】.  Retractable claws remain sharp for gripping prey and climbing【943641874497049†L123-L139】.  Many felids have colour patterns—spots, rosettes or stripes—that provide camouflage in their native habitats.

## Sociality and reproduction

Most cats are solitary, territorial animals; the primary exceptions are lions, which live in social groups called prides.  Territorial ranges vary enormously: a female puma’s home range averages about 140 km², while a male’s territory can be twice as large【671062261146697†L312-L318】.  Female felids raise their young alone; gestation periods typically last two to three months and litter sizes range from one to six kittens.  Young cats learn hunting skills from their mothers and disperse when subadults.

## Conservation

Felids face numerous threats including habitat loss, prey depletion, human–wildlife conflict, poaching for skins or body parts and climate change.  Conservation statuses range from **Least Concern** (e.g. caracal, serval) to **Endangered** (e.g. tiger, snow leopard).  Conservation efforts focus on habitat protection, reducing human conflict and maintaining genetic connectivity between populations.  See the **Conservation** section of the atlas for more details.
