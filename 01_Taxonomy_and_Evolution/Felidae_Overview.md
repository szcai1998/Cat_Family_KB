# Felidae Overview

![Feline Skeletal Blueprint](../04_Visual_Production/Taxonomy_Diagrams/taxonomy_felidae_overview.png)

The **Felidae** are a family of carnivorous mammals commonly known as cats. Felids are slender, flexible and muscular hypercarnivores adapted for stealthy hunting. They range in size from the tiny African black‑footed cat to the enormous tiger, yet share a recognisable body plan: large eyes for night vision, round and flat faces, sensitive whiskers, powerful jaws, retractable claws and a flexible spine. Felids walk on their toes (digitigrade stance) and their claws retract into protective sheaths when not in use. The exception is the cheetah, whose claws are only partially retractable to provide extra grip at high speed.

## Classification

Living felids are grouped into two primary **subfamilies**: **_Pantherinae_** (the big cats) and **_Felinae_** (small and medium cats). According to a modern taxonomy compiled from sources such as the Cat Classification Task Force, the _Pantherinae_ include the genera _Panthera_ (tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard and snow leopard) and _Neofelis_ (clouded leopards). All other extant species belong to the subfamily _Felinae_. In total there are roughly 41 wild felid species across eleven genera. Domestic cats (_Felis catus_) descend from wildcats and are part of _Felinae_.

### Big cats and small cats

Historically the term **“big cat”** referred to the four species that could roar (lion, tiger, jaguar and leopard). Modern usage often includes the snow leopard and clouded leopards as well. “Small cats” encompasses the remaining species in _Felinae_ such as the cheetah, puma, lynx, serval, caracal, ocelot and many others. Big cats tend to be larger and occupy the top of food webs, but the division is rooted in anatomy: big cats have a flexible ligament in the hyoid apparatus that allows most of them to roar, whereas small cats have a fully ossified hyoid and can purr continuously.

### Distribution

Felids naturally occur on every continent except Antarctica and Australia. Their highest diversity is found in Asia and Africa, but species such as the jaguar and puma range across the Americas. Many felids are habitat specialists: snow leopards patrol high mountains, servals stalk wet savannas, and pallas’s cats hide among Central Asian rocks. Others, like leopards and pumas, are highly adaptable generalists.

## Adaptations for predation

All felids are ambush hunters. Their musculoskeletal system emphasises short bursts of speed and agility rather than endurance running. Powerful hind limbs provide explosive acceleration and allow cats to pounce or leap great distances. A floating clavicle (collar bone) enables cats to squeeze through tight spaces by allowing the shoulders to collapse inward. Retractable claws remain sharp for gripping prey and climbing. Many felids have colour patterns—spots, rosettes or stripes—that provide camouflage in their native habitats.

## Sociality and reproduction

Most cats are solitary, territorial animals; the primary exceptions are lions, which live in social groups called prides. Territorial ranges vary enormously: a female puma’s home range averages about 140 km², while a male’s territory can be twice as large. Female felids raise their young alone; gestation periods typically last two to three months and litter sizes range from one to six kittens. Young cats learn hunting skills from their mothers and disperse when subadults.

## Conservation

Felids face numerous threats including habitat loss, prey depletion, human–wildlife conflict, poaching for skins or body parts and climate change. Conservation statuses range from **Least Concern** (e.g. caracal, serval) to **Endangered** (e.g. tiger, snow leopard). Conservation efforts focus on habitat protection, reducing human conflict and maintaining genetic connectivity between populations. See the **Conservation** section of the atlas for more details.
