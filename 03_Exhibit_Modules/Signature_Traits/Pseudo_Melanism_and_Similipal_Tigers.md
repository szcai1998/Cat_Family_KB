# Pseudo-Melanism and the Black Tigers of Similipal — Signature Trait Exhibit

> In one small forest reserve in eastern India, evolution has been running its own secret experiment — producing tigers so darkly striped they seem to be swallowed by shadow.

---

## Overview

Imagine a tiger whose stripes are not stripes at all — or rather, whose stripes have grown so wide, so fused together, so dominant across its coat that the orange background barely remains. In a normal Bengal Tiger, bold black stripes stand out against a vivid orange or tawny ground colour. In the tigers of the Similipal Tiger Reserve — a 2,750 square kilometre (1,060 square mile) forest refuge in the state of Odisha, in eastern India — this relationship is reversed. The black has swallowed the orange. The stripes have merged into broad, flowing sheets of pigment, leaving only thin islands of the tawny background visible between them.

These are not melanistic tigers. They are not carrying the mutation that produces a black coat in leopards or jaguars. They are something more peculiar: _pseudo-melanistic_ (falsely melanistic) tigers, whose underlying orange-and-black pattern is intact but so dramatically rearranged — stripes widened and fused — that the animal appears near-black at a distance. They carry a different mutation entirely, in a different gene, producing a different visual outcome through a different developmental mechanism.

And here is what makes Similipal truly extraordinary: this mutation, globally rare to the point of being almost undocumented outside this single reserve, is now found in roughly one-third of the tigers living within Similipal's borders. In one small, isolated forest, evolution's strange experiment has achieved a frequency that would be essentially impossible in a large, genetically diverse population. Similipal's tigers are, in a very literal sense, a population unto themselves — and their uniqueness is both a biological marvel and a conservation alarm.

## The Science

### What Is Pseudo-Melanism?

Pseudo-melanism (from the Greek _pseudo_, meaning false) describes a condition in which the normal stripe or spot pattern of a cat's coat has been distorted, widened, or fused — not through an increase in overall melanin production (as in true melanism), but through changes in the _spatial patterning_ of pigment deposition during embryonic development. The animal is not uniformly dark; it is over-striped. The pattern-forming system that normally creates discrete, spaced stripes has been disrupted, producing stripes that bleed into one another, merge at their edges, or cover the body so extensively that the background colour is reduced to narrow ribbons.

In tigers, pseudo-melanism produces what are sometimes called "wide-band" or "thick-stripe" tigers. The Similipal variants — sometimes informally called "black tigers" in local and media accounts — represent the most extreme expression of this phenotype ever documented in a wild population.

### The Taqpep Gene: A Pattern-Forming Switch

The mutation responsible for pseudo-melanism in Similipal tigers was identified through genetic analysis published in 2021 (Thakur _et al._). It resides in the **Taqpep gene** (Transmembrane aminopeptidase Q gene). Taqpep is a _pattern formation gene_ — it does not directly control how much pigment is made, but rather influences the spacing and width of pigmented bands during the embryonic stage when coat pattern is "written" into the developing skin.

The Taqpep mutation responsible for wide-banding in tigers is **autosomal recessive**: a tiger must inherit two copies of the mutant allele — one from each parent — to display the pseudo-melanistic phenotype. Tigers with only one copy appear normally striped.

Strikingly, this is the same gene that, when mutated in domestic cats (_Felis catus_), produces the "ticked" tabby pattern — a pattern in which individual hairs are banded rather than the body showing distinct stripes. The same molecular switch, differently deployed, produces dramatically different visual results across the 37 species of the cat family. This is an extraordinary example of _deep homology_ (the use of identical or near-identical genetic tools to produce different outcomes in different species).

### The Genetic Bottleneck — Why Similipal?

If the Taqpep pseudo-melanism mutation is recessive and rare, why does it appear so frequently in Similipal tigers specifically? The answer lies in **genetic bottlenecking** (the dramatic reduction of a population's genetic diversity when it shrinks to a very small number of individuals, causing rare alleles to become disproportionately common by chance).

Similipal's tiger population has been severely isolated for decades — surrounded by human settlements, roads, and agricultural land that tigers cannot safely cross. This isolation has functionally cut the reserve's tigers off from gene flow (the movement of genetic material between populations through migration and interbreeding) with tigers in neighbouring reserves. The population has likely numbered in the tens or low hundreds for many generations.

In a small, isolated population:

- Rare recessive alleles become **homozygous** (expressed) far more frequently than in large populations, simply through the mathematics of random mating between related individuals.
- An individual carrying one copy of the Taqpep mutation mates with another carrier — not because they are specifically related, but because in a small gene pool, many individuals are.
- Over generations, the frequency of the pseudo-melanistic phenotype in Similipal has risen to an estimated 25–35% of observed tigers.

This makes Similipal one of the most extraordinary natural experiments in feline genetics anywhere on the planet.

### Does the Coat Provide Any Advantage?

This remains an open scientific question. Similipal's forest is dense and humid — a landscape of sal (Shorea robusta, a large canopy tree dominating eastern Indian forests) and bamboo. In such an environment, the wide dark stripes may provide equivalent or even superior camouflage compared to the standard pattern. However, no peer-reviewed study has yet demonstrated a definitive survival advantage or disadvantage for pseudo-melanistic tigers in Similipal. The high frequency of the mutation is primarily explained by genetic drift (random changes in allele frequency in small populations) and the bottleneck, rather than active selection.

## Why It Matters

### A Conservation Priority of Global Significance

The Similipal tiger population is simultaneously one of the world's most genetically distinct tiger groups and one of the most genetically imperilled. Their isolation has allowed a unique mutation to flourish — but it has also eroded overall genetic diversity in ways that could compromise immune function, reproductive success, and long-term population viability.

Wildlife biologists have called for the establishment of **wildlife corridors** (strips of habitat connecting isolated populations, allowing animals to move and interbreed) between Similipal and neighbouring tiger populations in Odisha and Jharkhand. Restoring gene flow does not mean eliminating the pseudo-melanistic trait — it means giving the population the genetic resilience to survive into the future, with or without dark stripes.

### A Living Laboratory

Similipal also represents an irreplaceable natural laboratory for studying coat pattern genetics, population dynamics, and the evolutionary forces acting on isolated wildlife populations. Camera trap surveys (studies using motion-triggered cameras placed throughout the forest) have been critical in documenting these tigers, and ongoing research continues to refine our understanding of their genetics, behaviour, and population structure.
