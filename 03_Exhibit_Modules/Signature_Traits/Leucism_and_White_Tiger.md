# Leucism and the White Tiger — Signature Trait Exhibit

> Every white tiger alive today is a descendant of a single wild cub captured in 1951 — and that founding bottleneck has left a hidden cost written into their cells.

---

## Overview

There is a reason white tigers take your breath away. Against the deep orange and black that defines their species, the white-and-chocolate-striped coat of a Bengal Tiger carrying the double-recessive leucism mutation seems almost impossible — a creature that looks as though it was painted for a different world. Zoo visitors press against the glass. Children point. Adults fall silent. The white tiger is, without question, one of the most visually arresting animals on Earth.

But behind that magnificence lies a story that is as much about genetics gone awry as it is about natural wonder. The white tiger is not a separate subspecies. It is not an albino. It is not a purer, rarer, more spiritual form of tiger. It is a Bengal Tiger (_Panthera tigris tigris_) carrying a rare double-recessive mutation — one that, in the wild, would occur perhaps once in every ten thousand individuals and would likely represent a significant survival disadvantage in the orange-and-green world of the Indian subcontinent's forests.

Every single white tiger alive in captivity today traces its ancestry to one individual: Mohan, a wild male cub captured in 1951 in Rewa, central India, by Maharaja Martand Singh. Mohan was bred back to his own daughter to produce the first captive white offspring — and the cascade of inbreeding that followed has left an indelible, often tragic mark on white tigers' health.

## The Science

### Leucism vs. Albinism — A Critical Distinction

These two conditions are frequently confused, even in media reporting, but they are fundamentally different at the genetic and cellular levels.

**Albinism** is the complete absence of melanin production throughout the body. Albino animals have white or cream fur, pink skin (because blood vessels show through unpigmented tissue), and — crucially — **pink or red eyes** (because the retina, normally darkened by pigment, is also transparent, revealing the underlying blood vessels). Albinism is caused by mutations in genes encoding the enzyme tyrosinase (the primary enzyme needed to convert the amino acid tyrosine into melanin).

**Leucism** is different. In leucistic animals, melanocyte cells (the pigment-producing cells) are present and functional — but they are either reduced in number, restricted in where they migrate during embryonic development, or produce pigment only partially. The result is a white or pale coat, but with **normally pigmented eyes** (often blue, gold, or green) and **normally pigmented soft tissues** (paw pads, nose leather).

White Bengal Tigers display leucism. They have **blue eyes** — not pink — because their retinal pigment cells function normally. Their noses and paw pads are typically pigmented, not pink. They retain their chocolate-brown stripes, because the mutation only partially suppresses the melanin pathway — eumelanin (dark brown/black pigment) is reduced but not eliminated from the guard hairs that form the stripe pattern.

### The SLC45A2 Gene Mutation

The genetic cause of white coat colour in Bengal Tigers was identified as a mutation in the **SLC45A2 gene** (Solute Carrier Family 45 Member 2, a gene that encodes a transporter protein involved in melanin synthesis within melanosomes — the tiny organelles inside melanocytes where pigment is assembled). The specific mutation — a single nucleotide change (A477V, meaning the amino acid alanine at position 477 is replaced by valine) — disrupts the protein's normal function, causing it to fail to transport the molecular precursors needed for full melanin production.

The mutation is **autosomal recessive**: a tiger must inherit one defective copy of SLC45A2 from each parent (i.e., must be homozygous, carrying two copies of the mutant allele) to express the white phenotype (observable physical appearance). Tigers carrying only one copy (heterozygotes) look completely normal — orange and black — but can pass the mutation to offspring.

### The Inbreeding Crisis

In the wild, the double-recessive white coat occurs so rarely that two carriers would almost never meet and mate in a large, healthy tiger population. In captivity, however, white tigers became economically prized — zoo attendance surged when they were on display. To reliably produce more white tigers, breeders did something genetically catastrophic: they bred related animals together, including siblings to siblings and parents to offspring.

Inbreeding (mating between closely related individuals) forces previously rare recessive mutations to become "homozygous" (two identical copies) far more frequently across the genome. The result in white tigers is a catalogue of genetic defects that occurs with striking regularity:

- **Strabismus** (cross-eyes or convergent squint): so common it is considered nearly universal in white tigers. Caused by inbreeding's effect on the neural wiring between the eyes and the brain, compounded by the SLC45A2 mutation's known secondary effects on the optic tract.
- **Immune deficiency**: compromised immune system function making individuals vulnerable to infections and diseases.
- **Kidney problems**, **spinal deformities**, **clubfoot**, and **early death**.
- Many white tiger cubs are stillborn or die within days of birth — these deaths are rarely publicised.

There are **no wild white tigers** alive today. The last confirmed wild white tiger was Mohan himself. All living white tigers — estimated at several hundred worldwide — are captive-bred, and all are genetically inbred descendants of a single founding individual.

## Why It Matters

### The Ethics of Exhibition

White tigers are among the most controversial animals in captivity. Conservation organisations — including the Species Survival Plan managed by the Association of Zoos and Aquariums (AZA, North America's leading accreditation body for zoos) — officially oppose the breeding of white tigers. The AZA's policy states: "The breeding of white tigers, white lions, and king cheetahs is strongly discouraged due to the inbreeding required and associated welfare concerns."

A white tiger on display does not represent a conservation success. It represents a genetic endpoint: a population bred into a corner, its diversity so eroded that it can never be part of any meaningful rewilding (the reintroduction of animals to their natural wild habitats) programme. White tigers cannot be released into the wild, not only because of their genetic defects, but because their conspicuous white coat would make them visible to prey and reduce their survival chances dramatically.

### What We Should Celebrate Instead

The real conservation story of the Bengal Tiger is one of recovery — fragile, hard-won, and ongoing. Bengal Tiger numbers have grown from an estimated 1,400 in 2006 to over 3,000 today, thanks to India's Project Tiger initiative (launched in 1973) and community-based conservation around reserve borders. That is the success story worth telling.
