# Siberian Tiger – Behavior and Ecology

## Overview

> A solitary monarch marking the boundaries of a snow-covered empire, a ghost who tolerates no rivals.

The behavioral ecology of the Siberian Tiger (_Panthera tigris altaica_) is shaped by the vast distances it must travel to find sufficient food. Its social structure is highly territorial and dispersed.

## Key Information

- **Social Structure:** Strictly solitary, except for mothers with cubs.
- **Mating System:** Polygynous (one male mates with multiple females). A dominant male's massive territory typically overlaps with the smaller territories of 2 to 4 females.
- **Communication:** Scent marking is critical. They use urine, scrape marks on the ground, and deep claw scratches on tree trunks to communicate territory boundaries and reproductive status over vast distances.
- **Activity Pattern:** Primarily crepuscular (active at dawn and dusk) and nocturnal, though they frequently travel during the day in the deep winter to conserve heat.

## Interpretation and Comparisons

Because of the low prey density in the taiga, Siberian tigers have the lowest population density of any tiger subspecies. A male's territory can be up to ten times larger than that of a Bengal tiger in India, meaning interactions between individuals are exceptionally rare and highly ritualized when they do occur.
